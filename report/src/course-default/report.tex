    \begin{abstract}
        This report explores strategies for enhancing the efficiency of natural
	    language processing under constraints of limited training data and
	    computational resources. Traditionally, “bigger” language models, i.e. models
	    with more parameters produce better predictions than their smaller counterparts.
	    This approach of scaling up models usually involves large datasets and powerful
	    hardware, both of which can be expensive. The aim of this report is to analyse
        methods of optimisation that create a powerful model while requiring less data and processing power.
        This quality-over-quantity approach calls for high-caliber data in a distinct area.
        The dataset chosen for this work is the TinyStories corpus by Eldan and Li which is advantageous
        due to its fusion of diversification and linguistic simplicity.
        Among the optimisation techniques used in the model training process are thorough
        data preprocessing, a custom-made tokeniser and a curriculum learning agenda.
        In this report, these techniques are described and analysed with respect to
        their influence on training efficiency. In addition, the evaluation pipeline used in this process is demonstrated and justified.
        Our findings indicate that when designing a language model, size is not the only factor that can raise prediction quality and by implementing various optimisation techniques, small language models can generate remarkably coherent text.


    \end{abstract}

    \section{Introduction}
    A system which seeks to reproduce natural language has to consider a multitude of attributes:
    aside from providing an appropriate vocabulary, words need to be strung together coherently and follow the appropriate
    grammar.
    Each sentence observes its intrinsic logic and successive sentences need to have an underlying correlation that is coherent.
    In the context of natural language processing, it appears intuitive that a language model of higher complexity can fulfill these criteria better.

	In this report, we consider alternative methods for improving the efficiency
	of a model and aim to maximise prediction quality with strict constraints on
	dataset size and hardware power.
    More specifically, the goal of this project
	is to develop a small language model that is trained on a curated,
	small-scale dataset and whose training process can be conducted on a
	standard machine. The limited size of the dataset entails a need for
	specialisation of the prediction domain, so we aim to achieve high
	prediction accuracy in a specific target area. The model is to be designed in a way that it takes an input prompt in text form and predicts its continuation.

    Eldan and Li pose a similar question to this report's objective in their paper which first introduced the TinyStories dataset: “Language models \textellipsis\ often struggle to produce coherent and fluent text when they are small. \textellipsis\ This raises the question of whether the emergence of the ability to produce coherent English text only occurs at larger scales.” \cite{tinystories}
    The TinyStories dataset consists of roughly 2 million short stories generated with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 by OpenAI in simple English and lends itself to be used in the scope of this project. Eldan and Li argue that coherent natural language prediction has to meet two criteria: it should fit both the content and the syntax of its context.

    The first suggests a form of \textit{logical reasoning} ability of the model. Take for an example the sentence “Jessica was cold so she put on \textellipsis\ ”.
    The expected completion generated by a language model should relate to a warm item of clothing. The second implies the ability to use proper grammar: not any phrase that relates to clothing can be used as a continuation of the above prompt, but only those that adhere to \textit{grammatical and syntactical rules} of the English language.

    Building on these criteria, the objectives of this report are as follows:

    \subsection*{Reproducing stories}
    The model should be able to generate stories similar to those of its training set given a short prompt or no prompt.
    They should resemble the training stories in length, language and plot complexity.
    A model which achieves this objective demonstrates the ability to string words together coherently, fulfilling the \textit{grammatical and syntactical requirement}.
    Additionally, it is desired that the model display a certain degree of creativity and not directly replicate its training data.

    \subsection*{Logical inference}
    Secondly, a successful model is expected to be able to generate a prediction that fits the logic and content of its context. This comes into play when the input prompt is longer and provides more context, thereby restricting the realm of feasible predictions.
    This objective feeds into the requirement for \textit{logical conformity} mentioned above.

    \subsection*{Competitive with leading models}
    Finally, the model outlined in this project is desired to demonstrate competitive performance when compared to leading benchmarks.
    Specifically, it should perform as well as or better than the models presented in the TinyStories paper or other notable models on selected evaluation metrics. This comparison is an indication of the model's overall capability and of the validity of the methods presented in this report.

    \section{The Dataset}
    The TinyStories dataset introduced by Eldan and Li \cite{tinystories}
    comprises a little over 2 million stories of about 175 words length on average. They are made up of roughly 17,000 unique words.\footnote{number obtained by counting all words from the dataset that are also found in the nltk corpus of words of the English language\label{fn:firstfootnote}}\footnote{see appendix~\ref{exact_data} for exact numbers}
    For reference, the average adult has a vocabulary between 25,000 and 35,000 words. \cite{wordcounter}
    Children tend to acquire an extensive vocabulary later in their linguistic development, after they already have solid foundations in grammar and reasoning. This is the rationale behind this dataset, which aims to incorporate all the elementary components of the English language without introducing excessive complexity.

    The stories are generated using the GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 models and mimic children's stories with a target age of 3-4.\\
    Each story must meet the following requirements according to the authors \cite{tinystories}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item it includes three words randomly chosen from a set of 1500, representing a child's vocabulary
        \item the format and content follows that of a standard short story intended for children
        \item from a range of features such as dialogue or moral value, the story must incorporate a subset
    \end{itemize}


    These components ensure a definite dataset in a distinct target area but which still retains sufficient data diversification for comprehensive training.
    The prose nature of the data also aligns well with the core objective of language models, which is to process natural language.

    It is worth mentioning that the model designed in this report relies on this single data source for its training.
    While this approach might be less common, it is well-suited to this scenario because our goal is to generate in a restricted target domain that is accurately represented by the data used.
    Moreover, using a sole source of data feeds into the need
    for simplicity and allows for a more streamlined process of development.

    \section{The Model}
    Building on the transformer architecture, the model developped for this project integrates various optimisation procedures such as thorough data preprocessing or curriculum learning.
    These optimisations are aimed at enhancing the model’s efficiency and accuracy, particularly in generating coherent text within the targeted domain. Additionally, the model's performance is monitored through a set of evaluation metrics, ensuring that it meets the predefined benchmarks. The following section provides a detailed breakdown of these techniques and their impact on the model's performance.

    \subsection*{Architecture}
    The training and prediction process of the model occurs using a transformer architecture.
    Transformers are a type of neural network architecture which process sequential data using self-attention mechanisms.
    This allows them to consider the importance of each part of the input and capture complex dependencies within it.
    For this reason, transformers are particularly effective for natural language processing tasks.

    A 7-layer model proved to be the optimal balance between low model complexity and prediction accuracy.
    This configuration provides sufficient depth to capture the patterns present in the data compared to shallower models. Those struggle to generalise on the seen data
    and lead to results with an inconsistent flow of text as well as nonsensical words.\footnote{see appendix \ref{layer6} for a text generated with a model of only 6 layers}
    Deeper models not only require more memory and processing power, they also produce results of only marginally better prediction quality.

    Two embedding layers transform the input data into numerical format: A \textit{token embedding table} is used to convert the input tokens into vector representations
    and a layer using a \textit{position embedding table} encodes the position of each token in the sequence.

    \textit{Self-attention} layers are the core component of the transformer model. They allow it to understand the dependencies within the input sequence by weighing the relation of each pair of tokens.
    The model utilises 8 attention heads which perform the following functions: each head projects the input sequence into query, key, and value representations, each with outputs of dimension $8$.
    Additionally, a dropout with probability $0.3$ is performed. This final step aims to prevent overfitting by randomly deactivating a fraction of the attention weights, allowing the model to become more robust against overfitting.

    Following on from there, \textit{feed-forward} layers apply linear transformations and non-linear activation to the output from the preceding self-attention layer.
    The first linear transformation increases the dimensionality of the input's representation.
    Subsequently, a ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) function is applied, introducing non-linearity into the model.
    The features are then reduced back to their original number, followed by a dropout with probability of $0.3$ in these layers also.

    \subsection*{Parameters}

    The number of parameters in a model primarily influences its ability to represent patterns in the data. The more parameters a model has, the better it can capture elaborate relationships and details.
    The term “model size” typically refers to the number of parameters within the model.

    One factor which directly influences the number of parameters is the embedding size. This is a crucial parameter which determines the dimensionality of the space in which tokens are represented as vectors. Higher-dimensional vectors allow for more nuanced representations, but also increase the computational power needed for training and prediction.
    From training models with different embedding sizes, we can see that the influence of embedding size on running time is approximately linear. While we explored models with varying embedding sizes, a value of 256 was determined to be optimal for the purposes of this report. It constitutes the fitting balance between prediction quality and processing requirements.

    With this embedding size, the final model comprises approximately 7.7 million parameters. The authors of the TinyStories paper developped models of different sizes between 1 million and 33 million parameters.
    Compared to those, the resulting size of our model is on the lower end of the spectrum which aligns with the goal of simplicity.

    The optimiser used for this model is AdamW (Adaptive Moment Estimation - Weight Decay). The original Adam optimiser supports adaptive learning rates for each parameter by maintaining a running average of the gradients and squared gradients.
    Additionally, the AdamW optimisation algorithm applies weight decay directly to the parameters after the gradient update, which is useful in particular for transformer models. \cite{adam}

    This optimiser was used in combination with a linear learning rate schedule. From an initial value of $3 \cdot 10^{-4}$, the learning rate is linearly reduced to $98.33 \%$ of the previous value in each iteration. % <- reference code

    We used \textit{cross entropy loss} as the target function that is to be minimised during training:
    $$
    \begin{array}{l}
        \phantom{\hspace{4cm}}l(x, y) = \{ l_1, \ldots, l_N\}^{\top},\\
        \phantom{\hspace{4cm}}l_n = -w_{y_n} \cdot log \left( \displaystyle{\frac{exp(x_{n, y_n})}{\sum_{c = 1}^C exp(x_{n, c})}} \right) \textrm{ for } 1 \leq n \leq N \\
    \begin{array}{rll}
        \phantom{\hspace{4cm}}\textrm{where} & x & \textrm{is the input,} \\
        \phantom{\hspace{4cm}}& y & \textrm{is the target,} \\

    \subsection*{Data}
    The dataset used in the training process consists of short stories from the TinyStories collection described above.
    While the raw data is highly valuable, it contains minor inconsistencies due to its generation process with a large language model,
    necessitating some preprocessing to ensure optimal quality for this use case.

    The elementary data transformation process consisted of cleaning up those inconsistencies.
    Empty stories were deleted and through usage of a \textit{set} data structure, exact duplicates were removed.
    Subsequently, we replaced falsely encoded symbols such as “Ã©” with their corresponding UTF-8 representation, in this case “é”.\footnote{see appendix~\ref{replacements} for a full list of encoding replacements.}
    In some instances this was not possible, and the story retained special characters despite replacing the most common encodings. Those stories were removed entirely.

    This cleaning process resulted in a reduction to about 85\% of the original data. The working dataset contains roughly 1.8 million data points, the number of unique words remains largely unchanged.\footnote{see appendix~\ref{exact_data} for exact numbers}
    It might appear as though this reduction performed on an already comparatively small dataset is so strong as to hinder a meaningful
    learning process. However, it should be pointed out that this procedure
    was carried out with minimal alterations, aiming to preserve the integrity of the original data.
    Any data that was deleted was not viable for use, therefore the reduction of \textit{functional}
    data is significantly smaller.
    Taking this into account, the smaller size is reasonable
    considering this cleaning process provides high-quality data.

    A more sophisticated step in the preprocessing pipeline was the creation of a three-way split on the training data for usage in the curriculum learning process which is described in detail in a later section.
    Such a split necessitated a ranking system for the stories resulting in a total ordering. The curriculum would then be created by sorting the stories according to their scores and dividing the resulting selection into three segments.
    The close connection between training data and prediction purpose
    suggests that the same scoring technique be used for ranking the training data as for evaluating the model output. This also has the advantage that only one scoring pipeline is implemented.
    The exact composition of this pipeline is examined
    in the \textit{performance} section. In short, each story is passed to a large language model along with the instruction to give it a rating between 1 and 3 for its grammar, spelling, and consistency.
    The idea behind this automated evaluation pipeline comes from Eldan and Li, who used it to streamline the scoring process of the outputs generated by their models. \cite{tinystories}

    Creating this total ordering on the training data was a complex process that ran for multiple days on more powerful secondary resources. Despite the project's emphasis on minimising hardware demands, this one-time computational investment was a necessary trade-off to facilitate optimisation at later stages.

    Another important consideration when working with natural language is the choice of tokeniser.
    The \textit{BasicEnglish} tokeniser by \textit{torchtext} provides a good starting point for initial experiments, but proves too complex for our scope. Any large tokeniser requires more calculations and therefore induces
    longer processing times during training.

    Instead, we wrote a custom tokeniser to optimally handle the needs of this model. It is based on the byte-pair encoding (bpe) format and contains 4096 tokens in its vocabulary. Bpe tokenisers are beneficial in this use case compared to other types such as WordPiece or Unigram tokenisers because of their flexibility regarding
    out-of-vocabulary words.
    WordPiece and Unigram tokenisers attempt to split unknown words into sub-words contained in their vocabulary and may use a special \textit{<unknown>} token if they are unsuccessful. Bpe tokenisers more effectively handle out-of-vocabulary words by breaking them down into subword components that exist in its vocabulary, ensuring that no part of the word is left unrepresented.
    This feature was critical for the choice of tokeniser, considering the chosen vocabulary size is small compared to the number of words appearing in the training data.

    To further customise the tokeniser to this project's needs, it was trained on the TinyStories dataset, thereby providing that all tokens present in its vocabulary contribute to the learning process and no space is lost.

    \subsection*{Training}
    With all these features in place, the training process involved iteratively refining the model's parameters based on the data it is exposed to.
    The model was trained using a batch size of $32$ over the course of 20 epochs.

    Keeping the batch size at a moderately low level provided that the process remain accessible for execution on less powerful hardware.
    In order to draw on the benefits of a bigger batch size, gradient accumulation was used. This process causes gradients to be summed up over multiple, in this case two, batches before performing the update on the model's parameters.
    This artificially enlarges the batch size and allows for more stable gradient updates.

    One of the key features of this project is the implementation of curriculum learning in conjunction with layer freezing.
    In preparation, a three-way split of the training data was constructed. The first split consists of the stories which scored the highest in the rating and will be called \textit{top-level} data.
    The second split contains the stories that reached mid-range scores and the third split the remaining stories that scored the lowest. They will be referred to as \textit{mid-level} and \textit{low-level} data, respectively.
    The curriculum learning process entails exposing the model only to the \textit{top-level} split first, then gradually adding the \textit{mid-level} and \textit{low-level} data as training progresses so that for the final iterations, the model uses the complete dataset.
    Combining this with a layer freezing schedule results in the following process:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item for the first 5 iterations, training is conducted on \textit{top-level} data only and utilises all available parameters.
        \item after this phase, a portion of the layers closest to the input, in our case the first 4, are frozen and can no longer be updated during training. In the second phase, the model is trained on \textit{top-level} and \textit{mid-level} data, while only using the highest 3 layers, for the next 5 iterations.
        \item During the final phase, the entire dataset is made available to the model and all layers that were previously frozen are gradually unfrozen and can be updated again.
    \end{enumerate}
    The advantage of using this strategy is that in the early stages of training, the model learns from the best data which the collection has to offer. This knowledge is then blocked off and later iterations cannot pollute it.
    Finally, all layers are progressively unfrozen, allowing the model to gain a comprehensive picture of the data.

    \subsection*{Performace}
    Given the specific objectives of this report, selecting the appropriate performance measures is critical for accurately evaluating the model's performance.
    In a section above, the loss function that is minimised during training was already discussed. This section deals with the scoring of a model after its training process is completed.
    After training each model, we evaluated its performance by generating text and gathering \textsc{ROUGE-L} score, \textsc{BLEU} scores against both training data and validation data, along with two custom scores for the objectives 1 and 2.

    The \textsc{ROUGE-L} (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation - Longest common subsequence) score measures the longest common subsequence between the generated text and a reference text. Higher \textsc{ROUGE-L} scores indicate that a model has a good ability of capturing the general flow of the text, since the logical structure of ideas is retained.
    Figure1 shows the correlation between the final value of the loss function at the end of a model's training and their attained \textsc{ROUGE-L} score.

    It is evident that lower loss values result in a higher \textsc{ROUGE-L} score. The absolute values of around $0.165$ might appear low compared to common benchmarks, however it should also be considered that text generation occurs in a very specific domain which might be an explanation for the divergence of the scores. \cite{rouge}
    In any event, this figure is confirmation that the chosen loss function serves the purpose of this report.

    A similar case can be made using the \textsc{BLEU} scores on training and validation data.
    The \textsc{BLEU} (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) score was initially designed for machine-supported translations and works by measuring the overlap between the machine generated text and one or more references.
    In the scope of this report, it serves as an indication of how well the text generated by the model matches the training or validation data, but more importantly very high \textsc{BLEU} scores are a sign of overfitting. \cite{bleu}

    In Figure 2, the \textsc{BLEU} scores compared to training and validation data are plotted against the final values on the training loss and validation loss, respectively.

    Again, lower loss values correlate with higher \textsc{BLEU} scores, backing the choice of loss function. As before, the absolute values are somewhat low. \cite{bleu2}
    On the one hand, this can be a consequence of the creative nature of the task, as with the \textsc{ROUGE-L} score. On the other hand, it is important to note that the models depicted in these graphics generated coherent and comprehensible stories.
    To combat this discrepancy, we constructed two custom-made evaluation metrics that are more in line with the goals of this project.

    The \textsc{stories eval} metric uses a request to the llama3 language model to measure how well the first objective is achieved.
    From a randomly selected story in the training data, up to 3 words are taken and passed to the model to be scored. The generated text is then given to the llama3 language model along with the following instructions:
    \begin{center}
        \textbf{Please evaluate the following story based on the following three categories:\\
        Grammar, Spelling, and Consistency. Each category should be scored with an integer\\
        between 1 and 3, 1 being the worst and 3 the best. When rating a category, disregard\\
        all other errors except for those relating to that category. The output should be in\\
        the format of three numbers separated by colons, for example, '2:1:3'. Don't output any\\
        numbers below 1 or greater than 3! Output the scores only in the following format:\\
        <Grammar score>:<Spelling score>:<Consistency score>. Output nothing but the scores,\\
        no additional text or explanation.}
    \end{center}
    Note that this system prompt is adamant on the format of the output. This ensures that the result received back from the llama3 model is reliably conform and can be further processed in an automated manner.
    The final score is obtained by performing this step multiple times and balancing the averages of the three ratings.
    In contrast to the \textsc{ROUGE-L} and \textsc{BLEU} metrics, the \textsc{stories eval} metric is more aligned with the first objective of this report, which is generating coherent stories.
    By directly asking a language model to simulate the role of a teacher scoring a student's text, higher rating values become more representative of the quality of the generated text.
    Figure 3 shows the relation between the final value of the loss function and the value the corresponding model scored on \textsc{stories eval}.
    \begin{figure}[h!]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[scale = 0.5]{../data/stories_eval_vs_train_loss}
        \caption{\textit{stories eval score plotted against final value of loss function on training data}}
        \end{figure}

    On a side note, \textsc{stories eval} is the same metric that was used for ranking the stories in the training dataset.

    Similarly to a custom metric for the first objective, another one was devised for the second, which is logical inference. For this, the \textsc{logic eval}, the prompts are longer.
    Here, the prompts passed to the model to be scored are longer and contain a logical premise along with an example answer. One such combination prompt is:
    \begin{center}
        Prompt: \textit{Michael was very tired after the game, so he went to}\\
        Example response: \textit{sleep}
    \end{center}
    From a compilation of 100 such prompts, one is selected randomly and presented to the model. Its response along with the following system instructions are then passed to the llama3 language model:
    \begin{center}
        \textbf{You will be given a prompt, an expected response, and a response generated\\
        by a language model. Your task is to evaluate how logically consistent and\\
        appropriate the generated response is compared to the expected response.\\
        Please provide a score between 1 and 3: 1 is bad, 2 okay, and 3 good.\\
        Don't output any numbers below 1 or greater than 3! Output nothing but the\\
        score, no additional text or explanation.}
    \end{center}
    As before, it is assured that the result has the correct format, so that this evaluation can be performed multiple times and the final score calculated as the average of these runs.
    \textsc{logic eval} measures how well the model can perform logical inference from a longer prompt.
    In Figure 4, it is shown how this metric relates to the loss functions.

    By extension, they also served in the process of selecting and refining any optimisation techniques employed.

    Additionally, we selected the model with 7.7 million parameters as a representative for this report. The reason for this selection is that the model with 16 million parameters offers only marginal improvements in prediction quality, but at the cost of doubling the parameter count.
    Conversely, the 4 million parameter model demonstrates a significant reduction in performance.
    The chosen model scores $2.1$ on the \textsc{stories eval} metric and $2.9$ on the \textsc{logic eval} metric.

    In line with those results, text generated by this model is coherent and follows a logical structure.
    When given a short input prompt it continues it to form a short story. Names and pronouns are kept consistent throughout, and the plot is intelligible. For example, if an object is used in the story, it is first introduced.
    Moreover, the model can use the context of preceding text and complete sentences in a logically sensible manner.\\

    If the input passed to the model is an out-of-vocabulary word but one that follows the structure of a word, the model can plausibly incorporate it into its story.
    Take for an example the input prompt “Obama”. This is neither contained in the tokeniser's vocabulary, nor does it appear in the training data.
    A continuation of this prompt, generated by our model is:
    \begin{center}
        was so excited to go out and play. She grabbed her bag and put her clothes inside,\\
        and then she went to the park. When she got there, she saw a big, tall slide.\\
        She wanted to try it out, so she ran up to it and started to climb.
    \end{center}

    A more challenging test of the model's abilities is an out-of-vocabulary word that does not follow typical word structure, such as a random keysmash or a sequence of numbers.
    If the model receives a random string of letters such as “ASDFJDSIHSDF”, here is an example of how it completes it:
    \begin{center}
        !”, he said “I'm getting a haircut!”  His Mom said “That's wonderful, D”\\
    \end{center}

    which appears to be a sensible continuation.
    Entering a sequence of numbers does not always yield favourable results, and the model only sometimes continues on with a realistic phrase such as “years old”, otherwise it interprets the sequence as a name or outputs a different unsuitable continuation.

    The nature of this project's objectives would suggest that the generation of stories necessitates a certain
    degree of creativity while the deduction from a logical premise requires a more restrictive approach.
    Temperature, which can be seen as a indicator of creativity, is a parameter used in text generation that influences how confident the model needs to be
    in the next predicted token. From the list of possible subsequent tokens and their corresponding likelihood values, a high temperature allows the selection of a less likely one, while a low temperature restricts the selection to only the most likely next tokens.

    Intuitively, it would be expected that a higher temperature yields better results when generating stories and a lower temperature is better fitting for a logical inference task.
    Experiments with the model show that, unexpectedly, the opposite holds true.

    Setting the temperature to a value higher than $1$ when generating stories leads to inconsistencies or “too much creativity”.
    For instance, the names of the characters change throughout the story or the location changes without being introduced.\footnotemark[\value{footnote}]
    \footnotetext{see appendix~\ref{story_gen} and appendix~\ref{logic} for examples for both objectives with optimal and sub-optimal temperature values}
    Values between $0.4$ and $0.6$ proved optimal for meaningful text generation.
    On the other hand, when the temperature is too low and the model is given the task of logical deduction, it struggles to find the correct answer.\footnotemark[\value{footnote}]
    With the temperature set to a value between $0.6$ and $0.7$, we recorded the best results for the second objective.

    Among the methods that were employed for optimisation, preprocessing was one of the most effective. It is important to note that when using comparatively little data, the quality of that data is all the more significant. Cleaning up any inconsistencies allowed the model to only learn from favourable stories and thereby produce better predictions.
    Moreover, creating the three-way split of the training set constituted an important preparation for the curriculum learning process.\\

    Another impactful development was the implementation of a custom tokeniser. This allowed us to specifically define the size and content of the model's vocabulary. By extent, the resulting model is smaller in size while retaining the same prediction quality.

    Finally, the success of this project hinges on the implementation of a curriculum learning schedule. For a scope as restricted as this report's, building a foundational understanding before adding data complexity is a powerful tool in alleviating the training process.
    The schedule adds a structure to the data available to the model and compels it to focus on the highest quality data before expanding its knowledge to the full dataset.
    By using curriculum learning together with layer freezing, it was assured that the knowledge acquired in the early stages is not corrupted by later epochs.

    The graphical processing unit employed for the training process of our final model is an NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti and took between 5 and 6 hours to complete.
    This running time underscores the efficiency and accessibility of the model and aligns with our goal to ensure that the training process can be feasibly executed on standard hardware.

    \section{Future Work}
    While the development so far has already yielded notable results, demonstrating that the employment of optimisation techniques facilitates language models to predict coherent text with smaller sizes, there remain several opportunities for further exploration.

    To begin with, the loss function utilised can be extended to incorporate a cosine similarity term.
    This adjustment would further aid the model in embedding tokens that are similar in meaning into close representations, potentially increasing its capability to comprehend the essence of its training data.
    First explorations of this feature proved impractical, as they drastically increased processing time.
    However, the expected benefits of an implementation of a cosine similarity term suggest that if its computational complexity can be minimised, the resulting models would show better performance at smaller sizes.

    Secondly, we presume that the potential of the layer freezing method was not fully exhausted within the scope of this report.
    Further experimentation might reveal that there exists an optimal combination of freezing depth and unfreezing schedule.
    Finding this combination takes considerable effort and is therefore left for a future time.

    Lastly, the current configuration of model does not carry the ability to end its generation sequence.
    Stories from the training data which are shorter than a standard sequence length, are padded with a special padding token.
    This suggests that the model would learn this pattern and eventually indicate the end of its generated sequence by outputting repeating padding tokens.
    This however is not the case and it is desired that the source of this inconsistency be identified and erased, such that the model might learn when to stop generating new text.

    \section{Conclusion}
    At the start of the report, we introduced our three objectives that this project aimed to reach: reproducing stories, logical inference, and competitiveness with leading models.
    Through the implementation of a series of optimisation procedures, we developped a framework which can be customised to produce models of different parameter counts with corresponding degrees of prediction quality.

    The development process consisted of a valuable data preprocessing step where we not only prepared the dataset for training, but also sorted and split the stories within it with regard to their usefulness.
    Furthermore, the implementation of a custom tokeniser enabled optimal utilisation of the encoding capacity.

    The challenges associated with preprocessing the dataset while working with hardware restrictions were effectively managed, establishing the integrity and usability of the data.
    Additionally, secondary methods of optimising the training process such as gradient accumulation and refining the learning rate schedule contributed significant advancements.

    The most influential optimisation technique employed was the use of a curriculum learning schedule. By precisely manipulating the data available to the model, we were able to have greater control over the training process.
    Given the limited size of the dataset, maintaining high quality becomes even more crucial and this process ensured that the most valuable data was paired with the most significant part of training.

    Reproduction of stories that resemble the training data was achieved through a series of fine-tuning steps.
    From the TinyStories dataset, the model has learned to obey grammatical and syntactical rules of the English language and it is able to combine sentences in a sensible manner.
    Our first objective, which is to generate stories similar to those present in the TinyStories collection, can therefore be viewed as attained.

    More notably, the model has learned complex logical dependencies and is able to perform deduction when given a reasoning task.
    This can be observed in particular through the high score on the \textsc{logic eval} metric of our final model.
    We can assert that our second objective was achieved with the highest degree of success.

    Our third objective stipulated that the model developped in this project be competitive with leading models, principally those presented in the TinyStories paper. \cite{tinystories}
    When comparing those with our models of comparable size, it becomes apparent that they show promising results and can regard the final objective as substantially advanced towards achievement.

    Our results demonstrate that the optimisation methods employed in this report significantly enhance the performance of models with relatively few parameters, enabling them to learn from limited data and generate coherent text.
    The influence of these techniques is apparnet in our models' ability to generate meaningful output. Additionally, the successful execution of this project on standard hardware resources underscores their efficiency and practicality.
    These findings indicate that additional exploration of optimisation strategies could yield greater improvements in model performance.
